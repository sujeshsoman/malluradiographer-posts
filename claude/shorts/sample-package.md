| 4 | 0:40-0:52 | TR is the repetition time: how long we wait between one excitation and the next. Think of it as recovery time. Fat recovers quickly. Fluid, like CSF, recovers slowly. | Two recovery curves, fat steep, CSF shallow |
| 5 | 0:52-1:07 | Keep TR short, and we excite again before the slow tissues have caught up. Fat has recovered more, so it gives more signal. CSF hasn't, so it stays dark. That difference is T1 contrast. | Short-TR line cutting the curves; gap in accent |
| 6 | 1:07-1:17 | Make TR long, and every tissue has time to recover fully. The T1 differences fade away. So a long TR switches T1 weighting off. | Line moves right; curves meet; gap vanishes |
| 7 | 1:17-1:34 | TE is the echo time: how long we wait after excitation before we read the signal. Read it early, and very little T2 decay has happened, so T2 differences barely show. That's why T1 sequences keep TE short too. | Two decay curves; short-TE line where they overlap |
| 8 | 1:34-1:47 | Wait longer, and tissues with a short T2 have lost most of their signal, while fluid, with its long T2, is still bright. A long TE switches T2 weighting on. | Long-TE line; CSF curve still high |
| 9 | 1:47-2:00 | Put it together. Short TR, short TE: T1 weighted, fat bright, fluid dark. Long TR, long TE: T2 weighted, fluid bright. And long TR with short TE gives you proton density. | Three-row cheat-sheet table |
| 10 | 2:00-2:13 | On the screen, check the fluid first. Dark CSF and bright fat? Think T1. Bright CSF? Think T2. Don't judge by fat alone: it can still look fairly bright on T2. | Two images, CSF circled |
